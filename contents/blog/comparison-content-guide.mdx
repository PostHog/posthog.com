I've been chatting with marketing and growth leads at a few other devtools companies lately, swapping notes on SEO and AEO, and one theme keeps coming up: comparison content, and what a double-edged sword it can be.

Everyone sees the potential value, but not everyone feels comfortable investing in it.

The hesitation is usually some mix of (1) it feels unnatural to give your competitors their flowers on your own website, and (2) comparison pages come with maintenance nobody wants to deal with. Competitors ship things, change their pricing, get acquired (or acquire others), and all of a sudden the article you wrote in March is already wrong by June. Now you're the one spreading misinformation.

So how do you walk the line between being fair (complimentary, even) to your competitors while still capturing demand & showing off the strengths of your own product? And how do you keep the content accurate once it's out in the world?

Both worth figuring out if you want to write comparison content that doesn't get dunked on.

Both things I'm here to give some answers to, based on our experience [writing comparison content at PostHog](/compare).

## Why bother with comparison content?

Putting it plainly: because it works. Head-to-head comparisons (PostHog vs 'competitor') are ~8% of our blog's traffic and roughly a quarter of the signups we can trace back to a blog post. Add the "best X tools" roundups and 'alternatives' articles, and comparison content as a whole is a third of the blog's traffic and a whopping 60% of our content-led signups.

Since 2022, over 10,000 people have signed up for PostHog after reading a comparison. Three quarters of them within 30 days of reading it; nearly half within 24 hours. 

![Comparison content by year](https://res.cloudinary.com/dmukukwp6/image/upload/TODO_comparison_content_by_year.png)

Roughly one in ten people who read a head-to-head comparison go on to create a PostHog account. That's five times the rest of the blog!

| Content type (2025) | Unique visitors | Visitors who later signed up | Conversion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Head-to-head comparisons (PostHog vs X) | 30k+ | 3,000+ | **~9%** |
| Alternatives and "best X tools" roundups | 100k+ | 4,000+ | ~4% |
| Every other blog post | 250k+ | 4,500+ | ~2% |

The reasons are intuitive: someone searching [brand] vs [brand] has already decided they need something and are choosing between two. Comparison content is perfectly positioned in the funnel to help them make a decision (ideally in your favour, but not always and that's okay. More on that later).

## How comparison content evolved at PostHog

Our first one, [PostHog vs Amplitude](/blog/posthog-vs-amplitude), first went live in May 2022. It still gets a few hundred readers a month, which is more than I can say for most things anyone published in 2022. [Matomo](/compare/posthog-vs-matomo) and [Mixpanel](/blog/posthog-vs-mixpanel) followed.

2023 was the year it became a thing. Every new PostHog product came with a new set of competitors; [Session Replay](/session-replay) brought [Hotjar](/blog/posthog-vs-hotjar), [FullStory](/blog/posthog-vs-fullstory), and [LogRocket](/blog/posthog-vs-logrocket). [Feature Flags](/feature-flags) brought [LaunchDarkly](/blog/posthog-vs-launchdarkly), [GrowthBook](/blog/posthog-vs-growthbook) and [Statsig](/blog/posthog-vs-statsig). [Web Analytics](/web-analytics) brought [GA4](/blog/posthog-vs-ga4). 

We went from three pages to fourteen in a year, and the maintenance problem started flaring up. Keeping 3 pages accurate by hand is straightforward enough; fourteen, each with a feature table and pricing that changes often, is almost a part-time job.

In 2024 we launched [Error Tracking](/error-tracking), wrote [PostHog vs Sentry](/blog/posthog-vs-sentry), and watched it become our most-read comparison of all time within two years. 

**2026** brought the AI observability comparisons 

## Be the page you'd want to read

[Our handbook](/handbook/content/seo-guide) has a rule about this, and it sums up a lot of our foundational approach: most companies straw-man their competitors. We don't. Describe what they can actually do, assume they're reading, and assume they will call you out if you lie. 

PostHog doesn't have every feature every competitor has. That's fine. Our credibility with the reader is worth more than any row we'd "win" by fudging.
