I've been chatting with marketing and growth leads at a few other devtools companies lately, swapping notes on SEO and AEO, and one theme keeps coming up: comparison content, and what a double-edged sword it can be.

Everyone sees the potential value, but not everyone feels comfortable investing in it.

The hesitation usually comes from some mix of (1) it feels unnatural to give your competitors their flowers on your own website, and (2) comparison pages come with maintenance overhead nobody wants to deal with. Competitors ship things, change their pricing, get acquired (or acquire others), and all of a sudden the article you wrote in March is already outdated by June.

So how do you walk the line between being fair (complimentary, even) to your competitors while still capturing demand & showing off your product's strengths? And how do you keep the content accurate once it's out in the world to avoid spreading misinformation?

Both worth figuring out if you want to write comparison content that doesn't get dunked on.

Both things I'm here to give some answers to, based on our experience [writing comparison content at PostHog](/compare).

## Why bother with comparison content?

Because it works.

Head-to-head comparisons (PostHog vs 'competitor') are ~8% of our blog's traffic and roughly a quarter of the signups we can trace back to a blog post. Add the "best X tools" roundups and 'alternatives' articles, and comparison content as a whole is a third of the blog's traffic and nearly **60%** of our content-led signups. 

Since 2021, over 25k people have signed up for PostHog after reading a comparison. Three quarters of them within 30 days of reading it; nearly half within 24 hours.

![Comparison content by year](https://res.cloudinary.com/dmukukwp6/image/upload/comparison_content_by_year_ab710856d6.png)

| Content type (2025 FY) | Unique visitors | Visitors who later signed up | Conversion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Head-to-head comparisons (PostHog vs X) | 30k+ | 3,000+ | **~10%** |
| Alternatives and "best X tools" roundups | 100k+ | 4,000+ | ~4% |
| Every other blog post | 250k+ | 6,000+ | ~2.5% |

It makes sense. Someone searching for this style of content has already decided they need *something*, the only question left is which one. Comparison content sits at the exact spot in the funnel where that decision gets made, and your job is to help make it (ideally in your favour, but not always and that's okay. More on that later).

This isn't an AEO fad for us; we were writing comparison content BeFoRe iT wAs cOoL, back when the answer engine was a guy named Greg on Stack Overflow.

And while they have shapeshifted plenty since then, we've stayed true to two principles:

1. Do no harm. [We don't trash-talk competitors](/blog/why-attacking-competitors-is-dumb), we don't straw-man them, and we admit when and where they're better than us. [No sneaky shit](/handbook/marketing#no-sneaky-shit), as our handbook puts it.
2. Respect the reader's intelligence, and meet them where they are. A developer comparing tools is smart, busy, and already halfway towards a decision. Give them the answer as accurately and as directly as possible, and let them make the right call for their product and use case.

## Be the comparison page you'd want to read

PostHog doesn't have every feature every competitor has (*yet*), and we're fine admitting it. Our credibility with the reader is worth more than whatever we'd "win" by fudging the truth.

We're not the solution for everyone. We are [the best solution for someone](/handbook/who-we-build-for) (a lot of someones, actually), and the job is capturing *their* attention. Which means you will, occasionally, lose a deal on your own website: someone reads PostHog vs Competitor, concludes Competitor is the better fit, and goes and buys Competitor. We wish them godspeed and focus on our ICP anyway.

In practice, this means picking up a few uncomfortable habits:

### Acknowledge your gaps and credit their wins.

"If you need X, they do it better than we do today" is a sentence you'll find on our pages. Softening it with what's coming is fine (a roadmap link, a "we're working on it"), as long as you're clear about where you are *right now* for the readers deciding today. For example, earlier this year our articles admitted we were weaker on APM because we hadn't shipped [distributed tracing](/docs/distributed-tracing) or [metrics](/docs/metrics) yet. True at the time. We've since shipped a full APM suite, and the articles now say that instead. Our content matches our shipping, not just our ambitions.

Readers don't think less of you for crediting a competitor; they think more. It's the cheapest trust signal in content marketing, and almost nobody uses it, which is exactly why it works. Say the nice thing.

There's another reader to keep in mind, too: LLMs. When someone asks Claude "PostHog vs Sentry for a Next.js app," we assume the model pulls from sources it judges to be balanced. So be balanced. You want to read as a source, not an ad. Being honest used to be a brand decision (one we've proudly stood behind), but now it might be a distribution decision too.

### Write a "who should pick them" section.

If you can't write a fair paragraph about where your competitor wins and who they might work better for, you don't understand them well enough to compare. Go read their docs. It's also the place to be honest about who you are and aren't building for: plenty of our competitors are openly built for marketers for example, and while lots of marketers run all their reporting on PostHog (yours truly included), we're still building for AI-pilled engineers first, and we say so.

The reader who isn't a fit should leave feeling helped, not hustled. Honest pages also don't make enemies: the fastest way to go viral for the wrong reasons is a dodgy comparison, and if you'd rather not be bashed on Hacker News or Twitter for being slimy, be fair.

### Take corrections from competitors.

We've shipped edits because someone at a competitor emailed us, DMed us, or better yet, opened a PR directly against our blog. We welcome it and encourage it. (Yes, if you're a competitor reading this, this is an invitation.) 

Sometimes you'll be offered a slice of humble pie, and it's important to eat it gracefully.

My litmus test for every comparison: if a competitor wrote this exact page about PostHog, how would I feel reading it? Would it feel like they'd done their homework, or would I feel misrepresented and annoyed? If it's the latter, it's probably worth trying again.

We haven't always gotten this right, but we're committed to doing better every time.

## How to maintain comparison pages

Staying accurate across ~90 comparison and roundup pages for years is an engineering problem, and we started treating it like one.

### 1. Every competitor is a centralized data file.

In the posthog.com repo there's [a folder with one file per competitor](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog.com/tree/master/src/hooks/competitorData): `sentry.tsx`, `amplitude.tsx`, `hotjar.tsx`, and so on. Each file holds everything we claim about that company, in one place; here's a simplified version of what that looks like:

```typescript
export const sentry = {
  name: 'Sentry',
  products: {
    error_tracking: {
      available: true,
      features: { source_maps: true, issue_grouping: true, ... },
    },
    session_replay: { available: true, features: { ... } },
    product_analytics: { available: false },
  },
  platform: { self_hosting: true, sso: 'Business plan and above', ... },
  pricing: { model: 'Usage-based, per event and per replay' },
}
```

Feature keys come from a shared dictionary, so "session replay with network payloads" means the same thing on the Hotjar page as the LogRocket page. 

Most of our comparison tables render from these files through one component. When a competitor ships a feature, someone changes one line in one file and every page that mentions them updates on the next deploy. The repo is public, so competitors can see exactly what we say about them and open a pull request if we're wrong.

### 2. A Scout checks our homework

Data files solve "update once, publish everywhere", but they don't solve for "notice that something changed" – that's what [we've used Scouts for](/blog/what-is-a-scout).

Our comparison content scout is a scheduled agent inside PostHog that reads our competitor files, visits each competitor's pricing and docs pages, and reports back when what they say and what we say have drifted apart. A few things make it work well for us:

1. **It has memory between runs**, so it doesn't re-flag the same thing every week, and it learns what "normal" looks like for each site.
2. **It's selective by design.** Most runs end with nothing to report, and that's it working. It ignores changes that don't affect the overall comparison.
3. **It shows its evidence**: what their page says now, what our file says, and a suggested edit, with sources.
4. **A human ships the fix.** The scout posts a digest to Slack with a link to open a PR. A person decides whether the change is necessary, opens the PR, and merges it.

## Where we go from here

We haven't reached our final form yet (will we ever?). One of my goals this quarter is going back through all of this content and making it better, and I'm sure there's plenty we're still getting wrong. 

But the point is we started. Five years, 90-ish pages, and tens of thousands of signups later, "write honestly about the people trying to eat our lunch" has turned out to be one of the best decisions we've made for our organic content strategy; not the most intuitive one, but still one of the best.

So if you've been circling the plunge, take it. Be fair, be accurate, and let a robot handle the maintenance so you don't have to. (May I suggest [ours](/docs/self-driving/scouts)?)

And if you're a peer who's nailed this, or even better, spectacularly failed at it and has war stories and words of caution to share, [my DMs are always open](https://www.linkedin.com/in/nataliahelenaamorim/).
