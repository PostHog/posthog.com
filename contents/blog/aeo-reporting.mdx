In [a previous article](/blog/aeo-advice), I said "there has never been an easier time to be a charlatan when it comes to AEO performance".

If you are here expecting a retraction of that statement, I have bad news for you... A few months later, I actually think I was being generous.

Let me explain: [Answer engine optimization (AEO) is a fickle discipline](/blog/aeo-advice) riddled with questionable reporting methodologies, and turns out to be both remarkably easy to manipulate and hard to verify; that perfect storm has turned it into the hottest party trick in marketing right now.

The problem is that the confidence people express about AEO performance is, more often than not, entirely unearned.

Hot take? Maybe, but between you and me, some of the *"I'm winning at this"* claims going around have gotten a little ridiculous lately, and I think we should talk about it.

## Why most AEO metrics are just educated guesses in a trench coat

Every AEO number you have ever seen, from visibility scores to citation rate to average rank, rests on the <u>unverifiable</u> assumption that the prompts being measured are the prompts people actually type. 

This doesn't make AEO unimportant; at PostHog, it continues to be our fastest-growing channel driving thousands of sign-ups every month, and I'm not about to argue myself out of a job (not in this economy).

But there is no prompt volume data available to us mortals. None. No LLM provider publishes what people ask it, and nothing exists that plays the role that 'search volume' plays in SEO. And yes, I know about clickstream data; while I can concede it's the best bad option available, that's still different from being good.

Thankfully us marketers are resourceful creatures, so we do what we can with what's available to us: we proxy. We ground prompts in search volume, on the reasonable-enough theory that people ask LLMs roughly what they'd Google. We ask our own users what they typed. We do competitive research, social listening, the whole nine yards.

And while these may be steps in the right direction, they are still educated guesses stacked on educated guesses, not a scientifically sound methodology with a path to accuracy.

The industry is operating inside a gigantic blind spot, and I've been seeing a lot of tools and so-called experts talking about it as if it's been solved. Newsflash, it has not. Which is why "who's winning on AEO" posts give me chills, and not in a good way.

### "[Brand] is demolishing at AEO 🚀"

I get a debilitating wave of second-hand embarrassment seeing some of these on my feed. On the upside, I've learned just how far back my eyes can roll.

Before anyone comes for me: I am NOT saying brands claiming they have good AEO performance are necessarily lying or being dodgy about it. I know plenty of companies who are doing a spectacular job at capturing demand in the space, and they put their money where their mouth is with signup data, referral traffic, self-reported attribution, etc... you know, actual business outcomes.

What makes me cringe is when the **entire** proof of someone's success relies solely on a visibility score, calculated from a prompt set you chose… against competitors you selected… for only certain models, on certain days, at a sample size you didn't disclose.

The infuriating bit is that these posts are technically true. Anyone can produce a chart showing they're "winning" with numbers that are not necessarily fake (just lacking the necessary context).

Don't believe me? Let me show you how it's done.

## How to manufacture a perfect AEO score in nine easy steps

**1. Choose your prompts last, not first.**

Track a huge set of prompts across multiple steps of the funnel – some generic, some specific, some branded, some not. Run it. See where you happen to win. Delete or ignore the rest.

Now you have a prompt set where you dominate.

Need a boost? Put some branded prompts in the mix. You show up in every "[you] vs [competitor]" answer by definition, and because you're reporting on visibility rather than sentiment, you never have to admit which way it went #lifehack.

**2. Pick competitors who are bad at the thing you're good at.**

Nobody audits your comparison set. Pick the two competitors who are weakest in your strongest category and simply… don't include the ones that beat you.

If asked, say you chose "the most relevant players for these prompts." Nobody will follow up.

**3. Cherry-pick your timeframe.**

This one is free money, because these metrics can be super volatile; some of ours have swung more than ten percentage points in a single quarter.

When a line looks like a heart rate monitor, there is always a start date that makes it go up. Pick that one. Call it "the last 30 days" so it sounds like a neutral choice.

**4. Report only on the model you're best on.**

You have at least four or five to choose from and you'll perform differently across all of them. At PostHog, for example, we tend to do well on Claude and Gemini but have struggled on Perplexity and ChatGPT.

Pick the winners. Justify it with "we focused on this one given its market share and ICP relevance."

**5. Switch metrics until one flatters you.**

Mentions up but citations down? Report mention rate and call it "visibility."

Citations up but mentions flat? Report citation rate and call it "authority."

Both down? Report share of voice against a competitor set you get to choose (refer back to step 2).

There are enough metrics in this space that at least one of them is destined to be having a good week.

**6. Don't disclose sample size or run count.**

The same prompt can return different answers on different runs. This is free variance, and it's yours.

Run it a few times. Keep the good one. Nobody in your comments section is going to ask "how many samples per prompt?" and if they do, you can always just block them.

**7. Compare your number to someone else's number.**

Take your citation rate, measured with your tool, on your prompts. Put it next to a figure a competitor published using a different tool, a different prompt set, and a different definition of what a citation even is.

Present as a bar chart. These are not comparable quantities. It will look great.

**8. Present correlation as causation.**

*"We shipped 40 new pages and our visibility rose 60%."*

Did it, though? You don't know. But "we shipped 40 pages and then a number went up" is a sentence you can write without technically lying, and "50x growth" reads even better when you leave out that the baseline was 0.2%.

**9. Report on visibility and stop there.**

Mentions and citations are the easiest numbers to "move" and the absolute furthest from revenue. Being mentioned or cited doesn't mean anyone clicked through your product, clicking doesn't mean anyone signed up, and signing up doesn't mean anyone stuck around.

But "our citations grew 60%" sounds like a Certifiable Business Result™ to talk about in your next QBR with executives, and hopefully nobody will ask you what happened next.

## Can we all just be normal about this?

You have almost certainly seen a LinkedIn post or Twitter thread that followed one of these steps, or even possibly several, in the last months. I'm not naming anyone, because the point isn't that specific people are villains or intentionally gaming the system.

The point is that AEO currently has no mechanism to tell a rigorous claim from a manufactured one, and we should call it like it is.

To be clear, I am not saying we should be less ambitious or less enthusiastic about AEO, or god forbid that we report on it less. I may be biased but this is one of the most interesting things happening in marketing and tech right now, and being on the cutting edge of figuring it out alongside peers is an exciting position to be in.

I just think we would all benefit from a little less pretending.

And I am aware that's less sexy; the numbers you can actually prove can be ugly and underwhelming. Referral traffic is lossy, because half that bucket leaks out through referrer stripping. Self-reported signups are also gutted by attribution issues, and by everyone who can't be bothered to answer a form, which is a lot of people. It's not as fun to talk about, but it is fairer, and considerably less silly than pretending a visibility score represents something that it doesn't.

Reporting tooling is improving fast too; agent-level measurement (prompts running on simulated coding sessions instead of chat) is a meaningfully better proxy for how developers behave for example, and it barely existed a year ago (shoutout Gauge). MCPs have made it exponentially simpler to pull from five sources and reconcile them in one place. It's super cool, and I do believe we'll get real prompt volume data and comparable trustworthy data eventually.

But we're not there yet. We're early. Early is fine. Early is even fun if you're a masochist like me.

What early isn't, is an excuse.

The cost of being early is not knowing much yet; the cost of pretending otherwise is making claims that are, and I say this with love, bullshit.
