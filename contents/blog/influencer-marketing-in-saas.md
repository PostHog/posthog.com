Influencer marketing is the most underrated growth strategy in SaaS.

Over the last year, we've gained ~20K org signups and ~170K ARR from influencers and sponsorships alone. And our ROI keeps going up:

- In H1 2025, we spent ~$150k on creators and got 1.6K new orgs.
- In H1 2026, we spent ~$385k and got 4.8K.

That's 2.5x the cost for 3x the orgs, with cost per signup down from $96 to $80. Compare that to [non-brand paid search](/handbook/marketing/paid), which can cost us up to $1,700 per org.

Developers are way more likely to trust their favorite tech podcast or YouTuber than your crappy corporate ads. Yet most startups completely ignore it in their strategy because they have no idea how it works.

I've worked at TikTok and have been doing influence wrangling for years now, and I'm about to finish one year at PostHog as our first and only dedicated Influence Wrangler. Enough people have asked that I'm finally writing it all down in this guide.

## How to find the right content creators

Much like the startup world, the key to successful influencer partnerships is discovering and investing in talent as early as possible. 90% of influencer sponsorships don't even return positive in my experience. But the unicorns who make it, make it BIG.

That means I don't care how many followers someone has. We're in a niche, not-inherently-sexy category; if we sold skincare or sportscars, popularity would matter more. For developer content, I look for three things.

### 1. Are they being authentic?

As long as a creator's brand is related to software and their content is decent, they're already a good candidate for a paid partnership, with [a few exceptions](/handbook/marketing/influencers#sourcing-and-evaluating-influencers):

- We avoid channels about interview prep, career growth, low-level engineering, or heavy computer science, because their audiences aren't [who we build for](/handbook/who-we-build-for).
- Ideally, we look for web and mobile developers, product engineers, founders, and indie hackers.

You'd be surprised how many people don't meet the bar for quality when I'm ~~doomscrolling~~ searching for new influencers, though. I think it's because a lot of people who go into content think of it as a get-rich-quick scheme. To get views fast, they force themselves to be something they're not – louder, funnier, crazier, weirder – whatever gets engagement. But it comes across as inauthentic.

The most successful creators are the ones who genuinely enjoy… creating. One of the biggest TikTok creators of all time, for example, is [newt](https://www.tiktok.com/@newt). He's just a guy from the US who was living in a trailer with his mom, posted cooking videos for 4-6 years, and gained a huge following.

For devs specifically, a good PostHog example is [Chris Raroque](https://www.youtube.com/@raroque) who has grown to 100K subs now. He was already using PostHog and featured it in a video before we had ever spoken. We reached out afterwards, started with one small sponsored video in 2024, and have rebooked him every year since. It's always a huge bonus to work with [someone who already likes the product](/handbook/marketing/influencers#our-approach) rather than someone with a much larger audience and no connection to it.

### 2. The ability to create a conversation

Beyond the minimum requirement of not-being-cringe, the most important thing to look for in a creator is their ability to create a conversation (or, even better, a community) around their topic.

[Will Wang](https://www.youtube.com/@WillWang) is a great example of this. We've been working with him for about seven months now, he's a small founder in SF going through a funded accelerator and just makes videos about his life, family, etc. Currently he's at 20k followers, but they are a very active and engaged community. People ask questions about his founder experience in the comments, and he replies.

Even better is when someone sends a comment or message, and then someone else in the community – not the influencer – replies back. It shows that the audience is really watching and cares about the topic. We see this a lot in Will Wang's Discord where people look for advice or someone to chat with, and they ask and answer each other's questions.

This ability to start conversations is the greenest flag for me as a sponsor because it's what gives enough "activation energy" to move an audience member from just a viewer or commenter into an actual signup (and, hopefully, a paid conversion later on). You can put [a rough number](/handbook/marketing/influencers#sourcing-and-evaluating-influencers) on it. We treat 0.005 or more comments per view and 0.05 or more likes per view as excellent, and around 0.001 comments and 0.02 likes per view as weak.

![Influencers who start conversations turn viewers into signups](https://res.cloudinary.com/dmukukwp6/image/upload/q_auto,f_auto/Influencers_who_start_conversations_creator_sponsorship_post_0d4ef5f195.png)

Consistency matters too. Will Wang posts at least 1 a month, which is a good pace considering he has a full-time job. Chris Raroque, who has ~30k subscribers, is growing really well because he's consistently posting and improving his content with every new piece. What matters is that you have a regular cadence because that rhythm keeps people coming back, and the familiarity leads to trust and deeper engagement.

### 3. The "golden egg" personalities

After so many years of doing this, I've noticed a few personality archetypes who naturally do well as content creators. These are who most people picture when they hear "influencers", even though they're a tiny fraction of creators:

- People who are just **naturally very energetic**. In tech, this is people like [Theo](https://www.youtube.com/@t3dotgg) or [ThePrimeagen](https://www.youtube.com/@ThePrimeagen) – unfiltered, loud, engaging, funny, sometimes controversial. They have strong opinions and are not afraid to share them, and that's exactly what draws their viewers.
- **Subject matter experts who love to teach**, like [Fireship](https://www.youtube.com/@Fireship) and [JS Mastery](https://www.youtube.com/@javascriptmastery). Their content is high quality and easy to understand because they have so much mastery over their topic. They tend to appeal to broad audiences, so they're best for top-of-funnel awareness.
- People who do **"unhinged" meme videos** with MS Paint-style art, special effects, and weird voice tracks, like the [history of japan](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mh5LY4Mz15o) video. There aren't many people who can actually pull this off, but the ones who do usually go viral.

If you are lucky enough to discover them early on, congrats, they will lay many golden eggs. But also know that they are likely to increase their rates to match.

## How influencer sponsorships actually work

The other half of my job is the business side.

### Every partnership is a negotiation

Social media algorithms are opaque. Influencers don't know exactly how much the attention they garner is worth (no one does, really) so I handle each sponsorship on a case-by-case basis.

The goal, just like with any other negotiation, is to reach a number that we both feel is fair. Usually creators already have a rough ballpark number in mind. I can't share exact numbers unfortunately, so here are a few guidelines for what to expect:

- Prices and rates vary widely between creators since everyone has their own unique audience, range, leverage, etc
- A first quote from a creator we haven't worked with is [usually negotiable](/handbook/marketing/influencers#negotiating-with-influencers). Before agreeing on a price, find out what kind of slot it is (pre-roll, mid-roll, or full integration) and ask for examples of past ad reads.
- Meet creators in person when you can. One dinner in San Francisco got us a heavily discounted five-video deal and direct introductions to creators we would otherwise have reached only through agencies.
- Ironically, the more business-savvy the creator, the worse the partnership tends to go. Creators who pitch us aggressively usually feel less genuine and charge more.

Before the first deal, and again before every renewal, read through the creator's public accounts, including their replies, reposts, and older posts. They represent your brand in everything they post, not only in the content you pay for.

In terms of the logistics, pretty much all of this gets discussed over email, and that email thread basically works as our agreement. The creator will then issue an invoice that we pay via Brex.

### Flat rates per video are the standard

We almost always pay a flat rate per video, which is the standard anyway. Format matters more than the pricing structure. Long-form YouTube is still our strongest acquisition channel, while short-form on Instagram and TikTok does more for awareness than for signups.

This works well for both parties because you can never really predict if someone is going to blow up. For example, with Theo, we were one of his first sponsors and we've been working with him for three years now. When we first sponsored him in 2023, he was nowhere as big as the 575K subscribers he has now. People who came from his videos have generated like $180K ARR from 2023 to date.

We did try another model with ambassador programs but it didn't work. Or, at least, we haven't figured them out yet. We tried a retainer model called PostHog Ambassadors: a monthly fee in exchange for four videos a month. But I killed it recently because TODO.

### Longer relationships are better

Generally, the longer term we work with someone, the more value we get on that partnership.

Our best-value partners in 2026 were two mid-sized technical creators who started as small tests in 2025. They got bigger deals this year and kept their quality. Each customer still active after two to three months cost us $60–90 with them, against roughly $500 across the whole program.

Revenue also arrives late. Creator signups from the first half of 2025 brought in $8k in their first 90 days and $248k to date, so a partnership judged at 90 days looks like a failure.

The rest is [ordinary relationship work](/handbook/marketing/influencers#relationship-management): reply quickly, give useful feedback, stay in touch between campaigns, and meet in person. In New York we replaced a 120-person party with a 15-person dinner.

## A final word of advice

A final word of advice to share is that it's impossible to calculate *exact* ROI for how much your influencers are bringing you. Don't get discouraged if you can't measure it perfectly. The numbers I opened this post with are actually probably higher.

Marketing can feel like it runs on vibes, and influencer marketing more than most. People watch a video on their phone, then [sign up days later on a computer](/handbook/marketing/paid#how-we-measure-campaigns) by typing our URL, so the tracking link never sees them. For one creator, we counted 2,052 signups: 1,083 appeared only through the tracked link, 648 only through our "where did you hear about us?" question, and 321 through both.

But once you have run a few sponsorships and know what to look for, it gets much easier. You develop the taste to spot good creators early, and the only way to build that taste is to start.
