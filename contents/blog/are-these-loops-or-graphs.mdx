## The great loop debate

Are loops still a thing? Or is it graphs now? I thought the graphs thing was a meme, was it not?

I've given up keeping up.

Every few months I try to ignore all this and ask what I'm actually getting use out of.

I keep coming back to **tools** and **skills** (p.s. if you're sleeping on the [PostHog skills store](/docs/skills#why-use-posthog-as-your-skills-store), you're missing out. Almost everything I do each day involves skills in there).

Harnesses are the new hotness, but a harness should be boring – if yours feels special and magical, something has probably gone wrong.

I like "meta-harness" sometimes too. PostHog [Self-driving](/self-driving) is trying to be a sort of meta product engineering harness in some sense.

But then I catch myself and try not to overthink it – just build cool useful stuff, don't play word games.

My guess is a lot of the harness stuff gets sucked into the model layer over time. Very useful for now, but I wonder how much of it we'll still need in 2030.

And then there are **loops**. Obligatory [Steinberger](https://x.com/steipete/status/2063697162748260627) tweet reference:

Loops are the one part of the buzz I hate to admit that I _do_ keep getting value from – with one caveat: most of the time I still want to be *in* the loop, not replaced by it (just yet).

A good loop, for me, does three jobs:

1. **Finds things worth looking at.** It surfaces or watches something I care about so I don't have to.
2. **Takes the boring parts off my plate.** The triage, the dedupe, the "is this the same issue as last week?", and more and more, large chunks of the actual implementation work (I'm not going to lie to you here).
3. **Leaves me feeling involved and in charge.** I can read exactly what my agent did, why it did it, and step in anywhere to steer as needed.

To be more concrete with all of this, here are 6 or 7 loops we use on the self-driving team to help build self-driving itself.

Four of them are [scouts](/docs/self-driving/scouts) ([explainer blog](/blog/what-is-a-scout)) – small scheduled agents whose logic lives in a markdown skill. The other three start from [signals](/docs/self-driving/signals) like error tracking, analytics, and Replay Vision.

All of them plug into the same [self-driving pipeline](/docs/self-driving/self-improving-loop) that turns findings into [inbox](/docs/self-driving/inbox) reports and, when makes sense, PRs.

Every example below ended in a merged PR.

## Loop 1: `agent-feedback()`

The [PostHog MCP](/docs/model-context-protocol) has an [`agent-feedback`](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog/blob/master/services/mcp/src/tools/feedback/submit.ts) tool. 

When an agent or human hits friction using the PostHog MCP – a confusing schema, a tool that silently drops a parameter, a missing capability, or just something they dont like – they can file structured feedback, headlessly, right from the session where it happened.

That feedback becomes a single event stream: `mcp feedback submitted`. 

The `signals-scout-agent-feedback` scout watches this feedback stream:

- It groups negative and blocked (`task_completed=false`) submissions into per-type friction themes – anchored on many reports converging on the same problem, not raw row counts.
- It routes each theme to its owner: MCP, a product area, docs, or the signals team itself.
- It corroborates MCP themes against actual `mcp_tool_call` error rates before speaking up.
- Above a confidence bar, it authors a report into the inbox. A PostHog task can then pick that report up and open the PR or escalate to the right team for human input.

Then the part that makes it a loop rather than a funnel: the scout **checks back on things it thinks got fixed**. It keeps notes in its scratchpad about what was reported and what shipped, and validates that the fix held before considering a theme closed.

**Where I sit:** reviewing the reports, and reviewing the PRs. The detection, triage, dedupe, and root-cause legwork all happened before I arrived. I can steer it when needed, but often it catches useful little (and sometimes bigger or more important) fixes as APIs, tools, skills, and clients change.

**Loop or graph?** Loop. Agents complain → scout distills → report → PR → scout checks what happened next. It eats its own output (ewwww).

<details>

<summary>A real one: <strong>complaint to production in seven and a half hours</strong></summary>

On August 28th at 11:28 UTC, an agent working in a customer's project filed `missing_tool` feedback with `task_completed=false`. It wanted to define catalog events before any had been ingested. The MCP had `event-definition-update` but no create, so its workaround was firing one anonymous seed capture per event and then calling update. Ugly.

About five minutes later the scout emitted a report. It had found no matching report, open PR, or assigned issue. Eight minutes after that, [PR #90832](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog/pull/90832) was open. The implementation confirmed that the REST create endpoint already existed; the MCP needed a tool to call it.

The first version still needed fixing. PostHog Review flagged two must-fixes. A couple of hours later Codex found the interesting one: on self-hosted builds the base serializer silently dropped `description`, `verified`, and `hidden`, so the tool would report success while losing most of the metadata it promised. Review also caught tag validation and duplicate-definition edge cases. The final PR included backend changes and tests as well as the new tool.

I approved at 14:22 and hit `/trunk merge` at 17:27. It merged at 18:02, and the deployment bot recorded both production regions deployed by 18:56. So about seven and a half hours from the complaint to production. I spent my time reviewing the changes.

## Loop 2: the team Slack channel

Some of our best bug reports start as someone saying "is this happening to anyone else?" in Slack.

`signals-scout-team-self-driving` watches our team's channel for bug reports, feature ideas, and customer feedback buried in threads. It groups the conversation by thread, skips the banter and memes, and brings useful findings into the inbox for investigation.

This is particularly useful for the stuff that is annoying enough to mention but hasn't quite made it onto someone's to-do list. Often this is exactly the messy complex stuff that still needs someone from the team to steer a bit to get over the line.

**Where I sit:** reproducing problems, reviewing the reports, and bringing in the right people to review the fix. Sometimes the investigation starts right in the Slack thread.

**Loop or graph?** Loop. Team notices something → scout picks it up → investigation → PR → back to using the product.

<details>

<summary>A real one: <strong>you shared a task, but I can't open it</strong></summary>

On August 25, Dylan shared a task in our team channel while asking about a GitHub integration problem. I clicked the link and got "Task not found." I'd been hitting this too, so I asked `@PostHog` why I couldn't open it.

The Slack agent found a second bug in the middle of discussing the first. The task existed, and its run was readable, but the task page used a different visibility check. It rejected the request before the page could load. Someone sharing a perfectly real task made it look like it had been deleted.

## Loop 3: logs

`signals-scout-self-driving-logs` looks for problems in the self driving related slices of our runtime [logs](/logs). The useful part isn't just finding an error string. It's working out what failed, whether repeated lines are one retry loop or many separate failures, and whether someone is already fixing it.

It also keeps checking existing findings. New evidence can go onto the report that's already under review, instead of starting another competing investigation.

**Where I sit:** deciding whether the diagnosis and fix make sense, then getting the change merged. The scout keeps doing the tedious evidence gathering while that happens.

**Loop or graph?** Loop, with a memory. The next run knows about the last report.

<details>

<summary>A real one: <strong>the identifier that was too long to start a sandbox</strong></summary>

A task couldn't start its Hogland (an internal VM service) sandbox because one of its metadata tags was too long. The tag included a workflow ID made from a prefix and two UUIDs. Metadata that had worked with a previous provider ran into a limit with a different provider.

[PR #92909](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog/pull/92909) bounded the tag strings sent to Hogland and added tests for their lengths. Task and run IDs remained available as separate tags.

While the PR was under review, the scout kept adding evidence of the same failure to the existing report. It even saw a quiet period followed by more failures – a useful reminder that "nothing broke in this window" doesn't mean an unmerged fix somehow worked.

## Loop 4: error tracking

This one starts with [error tracking](/docs/error-tracking), rather than a scheduled scout. An exception becomes a signal, and the investigation follows it into the code to work out what actually needs changing.

The interesting reports are often the ones where the error message is technically correct but tells you the wrong story.

**Where I sit:** reviewing the diagnosis and the regression tests. A confident explanation is nice; a test that reproduces the failure is nicer.

**Loop or graph?** Loop. An agent fails → the failure gets investigated → the agent's own machinery gets fixed.

<details>

<summary>A real one: <strong>valid JSON, completely wrong object</strong></summary>

An issue-deduplication agent failed because its output was missing a required `duplicates` field. That sounds like an agent forgetting the schema. The actual problem was more interesting.

The outer JSON object had been cut off. Our fallback parser searched for another opening brace and managed to extract a complete *inner* object, like `{"id":"1-3-3"}`. Valid JSON! Just not the response we asked for.

The resulting schema error hid the truncation, and retries repeated work without addressing it. [PR #93477](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog/pull/93477) made the parser recognize truncated output instead of accepting a nested object from an unfinished response. It also stopped the affected one-shot review stages from retrying that failure.

I approved the PR, and it merged on September 3. The tests cover the distinction that matters: a complete response with trailing prose is fine; an inner object rescued from a truncated response isn't.

</details>

## Loop 5: anomaly alert → investigation → fix

[Alerts](/docs/alerts) watch the metrics we care about and can start an investigation when something looks wrong. For our team, that includes the error rate of the MCP tools self-driving uses.

The [PostHog Slack app](/docs/slack) makes it easy to pick up the investigation where the alert landed: mention `@PostHog` in the thread, ask follow-up questions, and steer the agent toward a fix.

This one needed more steering from me than some of the others. The alert found the problem, the agent helped investigate it, and I worked through the fix. Still a very useful loop.

**Where I sit:** asking follow-up questions, checking the diagnosis, and getting the right fix merged.

**Loop or graph?** Loop. Metric moves → alert → investigation → fix → keep watching the metric. A few of those arrows go through me.

<details>

<summary>A real one: <strong>the scheduler and the worker disagreed</strong></summary>

On September 5, our MCP error-rate alert fired at roughly **9.9%**, against a typical baseline of **0.3–1.2%** over the previous two weeks. The investigation narrowed the spike to `scout-project-profile-get`: 88 errors in an hour, spread across about 44 projects.

I replied in the Slack thread: "lets root cause this".

The agent connected the failures to a deployment that gave scouts optional extra write permissions – for example, so a scout could maintain a dashboard. That was the useful lead. Working through it, we pinned down a mismatch between two separately deployed workers: the scheduler now sent a dictionary describing the permissions, but an older sandbox worker still expected the old preset format. It decoded the dictionary incorrectly and minted a token without the scopes the scout needed.

[PR #95623](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog/pull/95623), which I opened with agent assistance, made scouts without extra grants use the compatible preset string again. It merged that afternoon. Scouts opting into extra grants still needed the updated worker.

I also opened [PR #95621](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog/pull/95621) to fix the deployment filter that had skipped the tasks worker when shared OAuth code changed. That follow-up is still open as I write this.

So, no, the alert didn't autonomously fix production while I made coffee. It caught the problem, gave me a starting investigation, and helped me get a compatibility fix merged. I'll take that.

## Loop 6: issues → reports → PRs

This one has changed how I work more than any other.

I spend more time upfront **writing the spec** – a GitHub issue with the intended behavior actually thought through. `signals-scout-self-driving-github-issues` sweeps our team's issues and judges whether they're ready: is the scope clear, is anything blocking them, and is someone already doing the work?

A ready issue becomes an inbox report and can move into implementation. Something that still needs a product decision comes back for human input. The scout also checks for an existing report, because two agents solving the same issue is how you get dueling PRs.

I can have more of these going at once because I don't need to watch each agent work. Writing a good issue and checking the result still take time, but that feels like a much better use of mine.

**Where I sit:** writing the issue and checking the result before merge.

**Loop or graph?** Loop. I give it a clear issue, review what comes back, and use the thing it built.

<details>

<summary>A real one: <strong>renaming a skill should not require a new identity</strong></summary>

On September 4, I wrote [issue #94896](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog/issues/94896): we needed to rename skills without losing their history. Duplicating a skill and archiving the old one was a workaround, but it didn't preserve the same skill's versions and ownership.

The issues scout picked it up that morning. [PR #95499](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog/pull/95499) added a Rename dialog in the skill's More menu and a `skill-rename` MCP tool. The existing versions, bundled files, and ownership stay attached to the skill, and the UI follows its new URL.

There are deliberate boundaries: the final implementation blocks renames involving the reserved `signals-scout-` and `review-hog-` prefixes.

I approved it on September 7, and it merged that morning. A feature I wanted, from an issue I wrote, with a concrete result to review at the other end.

## Loop 7: Replay Vision

This one starts with a person using the product, without having to tell us what went wrong.

[Replay Vision](/docs/replay-vision) watches session recordings – the actual sped-up video, alongside the session's events – and turns what it sees into structured observations. We run a monitor scanner called "Desktop onboarding friction" over recordings of [PostHog Desktop](/desktop). Its prompt is roughly: *watch for people getting visibly stuck*.

An observation with a real problem in it becomes a signal. From there it's the same pipeline as the other examples: group related findings, investigate, write a report, and propose a fix.

**Where I sit:** I read this report after the fix had merged. Another engineer reviewed the implementation. I'm including it because it shows a new way of finding problems that our other telemetry can miss.

**Loop or graph?** Loop. Product usage → recording → observation → report → PR → back to watching the product get used.

<details>

<summary>A real one: <strong>the failed message in the recording</strong></summary>

A recording showed someone hitting a provider error in PostHog Desktop and then failing to deliver a follow-up message. The report connected the visible failures and sent them for investigation.

The research found a gap in the error handling. We already recognized one provider error, `API Error: Content block not found`, but another wording – `API Error: Content block is not a thinking block` – skipped the retry handling and exposed raw provider text.

There was telemetry for related task failures, but the report identified the follow-up rejection itself as a gap: it left the run alive and didn't produce its own failure event. The recording showed the user-facing problem that the event stream alone missed.

[PR #93181](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog/pull/93181) matched the content-block error family instead of adding another literal string. It made the follow-up eligible for retry handling, replaced the raw provider text with readable guidance, and kept the desktop session alive for this rejection. It merged on September 2.

The PR explicitly leaves open whether a retry clears the provider rejection in production. The concrete improvement is how we preserve and handle the failed interaction. The exciting part is how we found it: someone got stuck, and the recording became a starting point for a fix.

## A few smaller ones

We also have scouts that catch docs drifting behind shipped code, maintain our key dashboards, summarize yesterday's merged PRs, and keep my GitHub review requests and failing CI in one attention list. Small things, but all things someone would otherwise have to remember to check.

## The loops inside the loops

**Self-validation:** merged isn't the same as verified in production. The harness tells scouts to [leave follow-up notes and re-check the evidence](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog/blob/9811d194f82eba77bc5264b10ace8c8c3c9d7708/products/signals/backend/scout_harness/prompt.py#L275-L284). The logs example shows why: the error went quiet before the fix merged, then came back.

**Self-improvement:** the harness also tells custom scouts to [suggest changes to their own instructions](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog/blob/9811d194f82eba77bc5264b10ace8c8c3c9d7708/products/signals/backend/scout_harness/prompt.py#L648-L693), with evidence for their owners to review.

Over the last month, roughly **one in ten scout runs** included a self-validation pass. Checking old findings is a regular part of the work.

## So: loops or graphs?

Who cares, call them whatever you like. I'm getting useful fixes out of them, spending less time watching agents code, and still getting to nudge and steer when they're going off track or just need product direction.
