# AI support

Unter's help page had a chat widget, and behind it a bot that answered most of what people asked <SeeFig n={1} />. That made the widget a product surface like any other. It had a cost, a response time, and a failure rate.

LLM analytics put those things beside the conversation itself <SeeFig n={2} />. Each question carried the model, tokens, latency and <Term name="cost calculation">cost</Term> that produced its answer. This meant the bot wasn't a black box.

The widget recorded what people asked and what they were told <SeeFig n={3} />. [Experiments](/docs/experiments) allowed them to test whether example questions helped. Did someone escalate to their support queue if they provided an example question and answer?

## When a person takes over

Some conversations ended with an escalation to a human <SeeFig n={4} />. A <Term name="user feedback">survey</Term> at that point asked the simplest useful question: _did the answer help?_

In February, the bot handled about forty questions a week and passed six to a person. The human answers scored higher, but they cost more. That gave Unter something better than a vague sense that the bot was “working”: a way to see where automation was good enough, and where it wasn't.

By the end of the month, the help page was one of the best-instrumented parts of the site. Questions, answers, costs, handovers and feedback all sat in the same trail.

But there was still a blind spot.

The data said what happened when someone opened the widget. It said nothing about the people who never did.
