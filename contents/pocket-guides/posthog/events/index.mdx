# Custom events and identify()

By January, Under could see clicks, pageviews, and where visitors came from. But there was one thing the data could not tell them reliably: _whether someone had actually signed up_.

The signup button was just another click to autocapture <SeeFig n={1} />. That was useful, but Under wanted the event to mean something specific, and to keep meaning the same thing after the page changed.

So they gave the action a name: `user_signed_up`.

## Name the thing that happened

The signup event was written in the code rather than left to autocapture <SeeFig n={1} />.

That small distinction matters. A button can move, change classes, or be replaced completely during a redesign. `user_signed_up` can stay the same because it describes the action, not the element that happened to trigger it.

The same call can also attach properties to the <Term name="person">person</Term>. Some <Term name="person property">properties</Term> can change later, like `tier`. Others describe something that should only be recorded once, like the campaign that originally brought them in. PostHog handles those differently with `$set` and `$set_once`.

Now Under had an event they could put in a <Term name="funnel">funnel</Term>, count over time, and still recognize after the March redesign.

## When a visitor becomes a person

There was another problem hiding in the signup.

Before someone logged in, PostHog only knew them through anonymous activity. After signup, Under knew which account they belonged to. The two histories needed to become one <SeeFig n={2} />.

That is what `identify()` was for.

On successful authentication, Under called `identify()` with the person's stable ID. PostHog could then connect the events from before login with the events that came after it.

The result is one timeline instead of two: the pages they visited as a stranger, followed by the signup, followed by everything they did as an account holder.

## Keep the ID the same everywhere

Under had a web app and mobile apps. A person could sign up on the website, then open the app to check a crossing <SeeFig n={3} />.

Both platforms sent their events to the same PostHog project. More importantly, both used the same person ID when calling `identify()`.

That is what lets PostHog treat those events as belonging to one person rather than one browser and one phone.

Without that shared ID, Under would still have the events. They just would not have the person.
