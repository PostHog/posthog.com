# What a fix is worth

By February, Under had one backend engineer and a growing list of things that needed fixing <SeeFig n={1} />. A slow form and a broken link both arrived as tickets. Neither said what happened if they stayed broken.

The answer was not in product analytics alone.

## Put the money next to the behaviour

Under already knew what people did on the site <SeeFig n={2} />. The warehouse knew what those people were worth: charges in Stripe, subscriptions, renewals.

Put those together with the other things the company knew — support tickets, the CRM, the issue tracker — and a ticket stopped being just a ticket <SeeFig n={3} />.

A host who never cut a hole might also be a subscription that never renews.

That is a very different kind of bug.

## Some fixes matter more than others

This changed what got worked on first.

A broken button on a busy page looks important because lots of people see it. But a quieter problem can be worth more if it stops hosts from ever getting to the thing they came to do.

For Under, one missing hole could mean a lost route, a lost host, and a subscription that never came back.

The ranking had to account for that.

## One number, wherever you ask

Revenue metrics were defined once and reused everywhere.

The number in the Monday email, the number in the SQL editor, and the answer PostHog gave when someone asked for it in plain English all came from the same definition.

There were six people at Unter.

They did not need a governance process for this. They needed everyone to stop arguing about which number was the real one.
