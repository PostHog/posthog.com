# Retention and cohorts

On Twig, guests filter stays, save places they like, and book trips. Twig's engineers record those actions in PostHog. When a guest signs in, Twig passes their account ID to PostHog to connect earlier browsing to that account. Their trend shows how many people use filters each week. Their funnel shows that 6 of 10 people who started booking completed one within 14 days.

Neither insight tells them what happens after a guest's first booking. **Do guests come back and book another stay?**

## Define a repeat booking

Edgar is one of Twig's guests. He used a filter, saved a stay, and completed a booking. If he comes back to browse or filter stays, he's a returning visitor, but he hasn't booked again. He must complete another booking to count as a repeat booker.

A <Term name="retention insight" /> counts people who perform a starting action, then a return action in a later period. Those actions can differ, but for repeat bookings, Twig's engineers use `booking_completed` for both. Twig sends this event only when it confirms a booking.

If Edgar signs in with the same account and books again, PostHog can connect the later event to his first booking, even across visits or devices.

<RepeatBookingFigure />

The inspector shows two `booking_completed` events for Edgar's account, each with its own timestamp. Now the engineers want to measure repeat bookings across other guests.

## When do first-time guests book again?

Twig's engineers ask: **Of guests who first booked in January 2025, how many book again six or twelve months later?** Guests may book trips months apart, so they use monthly periods.

They keep `booking_completed` as both events and count **Unique users**. They choose **First ever occurrence** for the start event so PostHog checks all recorded activity, not just January, to find each person's first completed booking. With January 2025 in the date range, PostHog automatically puts the 10 people who first booked that month into a <Term name="cohort" />. The engineers don't add them by hand.

This is a different group from the funnel's 10 booking starters. The funnel starts with people who tried to book; this cohort starts with people who completed their first booking.

They choose **On** to count a return only in the month another booking happens and **Period 0** to compare each month with the original 10 guests. This visual focuses on January. In a full retention insight, PostHog adds a row for each month with first-time bookers, so the engineers can compare groups that started at different times.

<BookingRetentionFigure />

Select a month to see which of the original 10 guests booked then. **Month 0** is their first booking in January, so it starts at 100% – it doesn't mean they returned. In **Month 6** (July), 2 of 10 booked again: 20%. In **Month 12** (the following January), 3 of 10 booked again: 30%.

Month 12's 30% means three of the original 10 booked **in** that month, not at any time **by** then. Someone can skip Month 6 and book in Month 12. Each person counts once per month, even if they book more than once.

The later booking must also connect to the same person. Otherwise, PostHog may count it as a new guest instead of a return. As later first-booking cohorts reach Month 12, the engineers compare that column across rows: are newer guests more or less likely to book again after a year? They compare completed months because a month still in progress can gain more returning guests.

## Compare people who saved a stay

Edgar saved a stay before signing in and making his first booking. PostHog connected that earlier `stay_saved` event to his account after he signed in. His example raises another question about the January 2025 guests: **Do guests who save a stay before their first booking book again more often?**

All 10 guests in this example have signed in, so PostHog has person profiles for them. The engineers inspect each person's events and manually add their profiles to two **static cohorts**: four saved a stay before their first booking, and six did not. A static cohort is a fixed list, so saving a stay later won't move someone between these groups.

<SavedStayJourneyFigure />

The visitor's `stay_saved` event on January 10 comes before their first `booking_completed` on January 15. Both belong to `user_202`, whom the engineers add to **Saved before first booking**. The January booking also places this person in the first-booking cohort above.

The engineers apply each static cohort as a filter to the same retention insight. Only the January 2025 row appears here because the two static cohorts contain only those 10 guests. The filter changes which guests appear in that row. Select a month in the table to see who counted. In Month 12 (January 2026), `user_202`'s January 18 `booking_completed` counts as a return.

<SavedCohortRetentionFigure />

In the saved group, `user_202` and `user_203` booked again: 2 of 4, or 50%. In the other group, only `user_205` did: 1 of 6, or about 17%. Together, they're the three returning guests in the January cohort above. The percentages compare groups of different sizes; the counts show how small this example is.

To see whether this difference holds beyond January, the engineers would add first-time bookers from later months to the appropriate static cohort. Then they could compare saved and unsaved guests in each row once its Month 12 period is complete. If the gap appears across several rows, it's a pattern worth investigating. January alone is too small to draw a conclusion.

Even a repeated gap wouldn't prove saving a stay caused another booking. People who travel often might be more likely to save stays and book again. The engineers inspect event histories and any available replays before deciding whether to change Twig.

Next, the engineers put these insights on a dashboard. The team can see filter use, booking completion, and repeat bookings together before choosing what to investigate.
