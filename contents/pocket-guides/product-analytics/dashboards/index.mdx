# Dashboards

Twig's engineers have recorded filter selections, connected visits to signed-in guests, and built insights for filter use, booking completion, and repeat bookings. Now they need a way to view that data together and use it to decide what to build next.

A dashboard puts saved insights on one page. Twig starts with an overview, then builds two dashboards to answer narrower questions.

## Build an overview dashboard

The engineers build their first dashboard, and name it **Exploring and booking stays**. They add three saved insights: their trends insight for weekly filter use, funnels insight for booking completion, and retention insight for repeat bookings:

<BookingDashboardFigure />

Adding the funnel to this dashboard shows Twig's note about failed requests and booking trails to inspect. This gives the engineers a path to go down if they notice something "off" in their dashboard. The tiles use different measures: filter users per week, booking completion within 14 days, and repeat bookings by month – so the engineers leave **No date range override** on so each saved insight keeps its own dates and interval.

## Build a drill-down dashboard

Twig's Trends insight shows more people using filters in week 2, but it doesn't show which destinations they chose. The engineers create a **Finding stays** dashboard and put the destination breakdown beside the weekly trend:

<FindingStaysDashboardFigure />

The destination tile shows five example clicks: three for Coast and one each for Forest and City. The trend counts five people in week 1 and eight in week 2. The clicks are a different sample, so they cannot explain the weekly rise. The engineers can inspect selections with `has_results: false` to find filters that returned no stays.

## Compare cohorts side by side

Twig also wants to know whether guests who saved a stay before their first booking return more often. The engineers build a **Returning guests** dashboard with three views of January 2025: all first-time bookers, the four who saved a stay, and the six who didn't. The last two use their static cohorts as filters:

<ReturningGuestsDashboardFigure />

Of the 10 January guests, three booked again in Month 12. Two of four in the saved group did (50%); one of six in the other group did (17%). This small comparison doesn't show that saving caused another booking. The engineers would compare later first-booking cohorts after they reach Month 12.

## Use dashboards as evidence

The booking funnel gives the engineers an immediate lead. Of 10 people who started booking, four didn't finish within 14 days and one had a failed request. Twig's `request_id` connects that person's `booking_started` event to the server response. The engineers inspect the other three booking trails and the failed response before deciding what to fix. A <Term name="session replay">Session Replay</Term> can show what a visitor saw, but not why the server request failed.

After a booking-flow change, they check the funnel again after a full 14-day conversion window, keeping its events, order, and filters the same. A higher completion rate means more starters finished within that window. It doesn't prove their change caused the increase – they could test that with <Term name="experiment">Experiments</Term>.
