# Configure replays

Two things have to be true before a session is recorded: a PostHog SDK is installed with session recording enabled, and recording is switched on in your project settings. Once both are, the library starts recording sessions by default.

Recording everything is rarely what you want, and it's the main driver of cost.

## Set the rules before the traffic

Recording rules apply at capture time, so they only affect sessions recorded after you change them. A session you recorded at the wrong setting stays recorded that way.

Start by capturing 100% of sessions and decrease from there. That's the fastest way to see how many sessions you're recording and how much data you're collecting.

## Sampling

Sampling records a percentage of all sessions. It's deterministic: when a session starts, PostHog hashes the session ID into a number between 0 and 1 and compares it to your sample rate. Under the rate, the session is recorded.

Because the same session ID always produces the same number, the decision holds for the whole session, including across page refreshes. What you can't do is choose *which* sessions get picked – the hash decides.

## Triggers

You can also record on a condition instead of a coin flip: a URL, an event (an exception, say), a feature flag, or a <Term name="cohort" />. Trigger groups are the flexible form – each group carries its own URLs, events, flags, sample rate, minimum duration, and match type, and a session is recorded if it matches any group.

One trap worth knowing: "any" matching with 100% sampling records every session, and your other conditions stop mattering – the sampling condition alone matches everything. Lower the rate or switch to "all" matching.

## Minimum duration

A minimum duration drops sessions too short to be useful – under two seconds, say, where someone bounced before the page settled.

## The defaults

Any input element is highly likely to contain sensitive text, so inputs are masked by default. General text is not.

That asymmetry is what surprises people. If your app renders an email address, an invoice, or someone's name as ordinary text, it's in the replay until you say otherwise.

You can find the details in this reference page.

## Masking more

Three levers, roughly in order of bluntness. `maskTextSelector: "*"` masks all non-input text. A CSS selector – `.email, #sensitive` – masks specific elements, and masking an element applies to its children, so `:not` selectors don't do what you want. And `maskTextFn` decides per string, which is how you mask only text that looks like an email.

For anything that isn't text or an input, add the class `ph-no-capture`. The element is replaced by a block of the same size in playback – tell your team, so nobody reports the blank rectangle as a bug. It also stops <Term name="autocapture" /> events firing from that element.

## Masking by exception

The inverse is often the safer posture: mask everything, then unmask what you've confirmed is safe. Set `maskAllInputs` and `maskTextSelector: "*"`, then let `maskTextFn` reveal only elements carrying a data attribute you control.

## The URL bar counts

The replay player shows the page URL on the timeline, and query strings carry auth tokens and user identifiers. The `maskCapturedNetworkRequestFn` callback runs against the captured page URL as well as network requests, so one config redacts both – in the browser, before anything is sent.

## The browser panels

[Console logs](/docs/session-replay/console-log-recording) and [network requests](/docs/session-replay/network-recording) are opt-in, so those tabs in the inspector are only as full as your capture config. The [Network waterfall](/docs/session-replay/how-to-watch-recordings) tab adds the page performance score and asset breakdown.

## Mobile, and network bodies

Native Android and iOS record a wireframe of the view hierarchy; screenshot mode captures the real screen instead, which means it can capture anything on it. React Native and Flutter always record in screenshot mode, so masking there isn't optional.

Request and response bodies are a switch of their own, and off for exactly this reason: turning network capture on doesn't turn them on with it.

## How long it's kept

Retention is a project setting, and the ceiling depends on your plan: 30 days on Free, 90 on pay-as-you-go, a year on Boost and Scale, up to five years on Enterprise.

Changing it only affects recordings captured from that point on – existing recordings keep the retention they were captured with. Once a recording is deleted it can't be restored, which is the point if you're subject to data protection regulations. To keep one past its expiry, export it to JSON.

## What this costs you if you skip it

When Session replay is enabled it will capture all sessions by default. As every session is counted for billing purposes, customers may end up with a bunch of low value short recordings and still be paying for them.

If a customer has Session replay enabled, log in as them and look at their session replay [settings](/docs/session-replay/how-to-control-which-sessions-you-record). At a minimum we recommend setting the minimum duration to 2 seconds or more but there are other tuning options which they may also benefit from.

## What this costs you

Session replay is free up to a monthly allowance of web and mobile recordings, then priced per recording. Recording rules and data retention are the two biggest levers on the bill, and you can set a billing limit so a traffic spike can't surprise you.
