**A/B testing** compares two versions of a product by showing each version to a randomly assigned group of people and measuring which group performs better against a metric chosen in advance. It is also called split testing. A test with more than two versions is usually called multivariate testing.

The method is a randomised controlled trial applied to software. Random assignment is the whole mechanism: it makes the two groups comparable in every respect except the one being tested, so any difference in the result can be attributed to the change.

## Why it is necessary

The obvious alternative – ship the change and watch the metric – measures the change and the passage of time together. A metric moves for many reasons at once: the day of the week, a campaign, a holiday, a competitor, an unrelated release. Before-and-after comparison cannot separate them.

Running both versions simultaneously on comparable groups removes the confounding, because every other influence applies to both groups equally.

## How a test is constructed

1. **State the hypothesis first.** What change, which metric, in which direction. A hypothesis written after the result is not a hypothesis.
2. **Choose one primary metric.** More than one primary metric means more than one chance of a positive result, and the test loses its discipline.
3. **Assign people at random, and stably.** A person must see the same variant every time, or the comparison measures nothing.
4. **Set the duration in advance.** This is the part most often skipped, and the reason is the next section.
5. **Watch for damage elsewhere.** Secondary metrics catch a change that improves one number by harming another.

## Common errors

**Stopping when it looks good.** A metric checked repeatedly will eventually cross a significance threshold by chance. Deciding to stop because the result is currently positive converts noise into a conclusion. The duration must be fixed before the test begins.

**Too little traffic.** Detecting a small difference requires a large sample. A test on low traffic does not produce a weak answer; it produces no answer, whatever the interface displays.

**Testing something trivial.** A test costs time and traffic. Spending both on a button color is a real cost with a small possible payoff.

**Measuring the click and not the outcome.** A variant that increases clicks and decreases completions has made the product worse.
